Overview
Earthbound gryphons came into Virath’s mountains after the Unveiling. They stand a destrier’s height at the shoulder and outweigh one: eagle head and beak, golden eyes, lion’s body, tawny hide. Shoulder feathers give a killing leap, not flight. They are proud, long-memoried, and will fight to the death for a rider who has stood the trials. They are not the winged Royal Gryphon.

Signs
- Talon-scored stone on ledges and nest-crags
- Nests of wedged timber and piled stone under an overhang
- Goat, boar, or traveller taken and carried onto height
- Loose rock, cairns, and climber-gear knocked into the drop
- Gargoyles or roof-beasts missing from high stone
- Roar off the massif, louder under a False Moon
- A single bird far below its range, following a party for days
- Sheep, cattle, or horse-lines broken in the night, the kills carried or torn, no pack-trail

Behaviour
A pair holds a territory for life: the nest-crag, the faces around it, and the steep wood that hangs off the same height. They do not migrate between countries. They move up and down the mountain. Highsun they hunt the high snow-edge. Longnight they drop to the last open ledges and the heads of hanging valleys. Steep woodland is hunting country, not nest country. They use it when it sits against their own rock. A False Moon pulls them lower still, into places men walk.

They hunt at first light and last light. The leap from a standing start will clear a cart and come down through plate. They cache on the nest-ledge and return. In idle weather they bat stones, bones, and anything loose into the drop. This is play, not signalling.

They pair for life, including the years after they have stopped breeding. Courtship is Thaw: shared hunts, exchanged kills, and long standing on the same rock. They do not keep a pride. Once the territory is taken they keep it until they are expelled. They are fierce on the boundary. Expulsion is by a stronger pair, not by wandering off.

A range without a mate is a young bird still roaming, or a widow. Widows do not nest. If there are still young on the crag, the remaining adult keeps the territory and finishes the rearing. If there are no young, the widow or widower does not hold the rock. It leaves and roams, far beyond the old boundary, sometimes for the rest of its life. Those birds turn up on roads, in vale pasture, and on the roofs of places that have never seen a nest.

They grieve. A slain mate or a slain chick is not eaten and is not abandoned at once. The survivor will stay by the body, then hunt the killers. That hunt does not respect the old boundary. Bands that took a mate have been followed off the mountain, through the steep wood, and onto roads the pair never used. The stalking may last days.

Destructive raids on human holdings — a fold broken, a herd dropped and left — are believed to happen only when humans did the first harm. The bird is settling a debt, not feeding. A widow that was not wronged by men does not do this. She roams, hunts as she goes, and takes from a herd only what she will eat. She is an opportunistic hunter on the road, not a scourge.

Folk still blame every lone gryphon in the low country. The meat-left-behind is the mark of a wronged widow. A single sheep gone, cleanly, is only hunger.

The nest is built, not scraped. Timber and stone are wedged into an inset cliff with rock or tree-cover overhead. One egg, sometimes two. Both sit. Both kill anything that comes up the face. The young are raised on the nest and on the home faces until young adulthood, then they leave of their own will. They roam, sometimes for years, until they pair and take a territory of their own. Most die in that interval. A bird that has taken a crag is already a full hunter. They breed at four to six.

Knights Gryphon take the egg, not the adult. The pair treats that as the killing of young and will come down the mountain. Hand-reared birds bond as a mate-substitute: one rider, for life. A broken bond — death, cruelty, abandonment — does not make a safe beast. Wild adults do not take a master.

Encounter
Usually one hunting, or the pair on the nest-crag. Two eggs is a bad year to climb.

A lone adult well below the massif, matching a party’s route, is likely vengeance, not a stray.
A lone adult on the vale, taking one head from a herd and moving on, is likely a widow without young, hunting.
A lone adult that wrecks a fold and leaves the meat is likely a widow that men already wronged.

A bonded mount is a charge on the ground, not a stoop from the air.

Quantity stays low (Rarity 4). Unveiling weather raises likelihood and how far they descend.
