THE NOTES
--------------------------------------------------

Of all the delusions that haunt the frontier, none is
so persistent, yet so thinly documented, as the myth
of the Still-born. In the common taverns of the
lowlands, they are spoken of in whispers alongside
skinwights and bog-sprites — creatures of the
campfire rather than the ledger. Indeed, their very
existence is mathematically improbable, for they
rarely outlive the cradle.

Throughout my travels across the mountain rims, I
have recorded nineteen distinct instances of a
singular maternal malady, which the local mid-wives
name The Mother’s Chill, or The Milk-Souring. It
manifests not as a fever of the blood, but as a
sudden, unnatural horror of the newborn. In every
account, a mother — having delivered a physically
unblemished child — will flatly refuse to touch or
nourish it. They describe a creeping nausea in its
proximity, an instinctual dread that mimics a
profound, unnatural madness of the mind. Abandoned
by the very breast that should give them life,
these wretched babes almost invariably wither
before their first Thaw.

Yet, a thread of speculation remains among the
older text-fragments. In a ruined ledger from some
forgotten, long-dead kingdom, I found a single word
scrawled beside a census deficit: Atraxi. The
scribe offered no definition, but the root hints at
an ancient tongue — a word for absolute, unbothered
stillness.

The accounts I have spent years collecting from
far-flung settlements offer no consensus on what an
Atraxi truly is. The isolated forest-dwellers of
the high plateau swear they are individuals whose
bloodlines were clogged with dead earth when the
first ancestors fell from the sky, leaving them
spiritually deaf to the world. Conversely, the
deep-miners of the western peaks claim they are not
cursed at all, but rather men born with
"iron-minds" that are simply too dense to feel the
weight of regular prayer. It is a tapestry of
contradictions and old grandmother's tales.

I, too, shared the common scholarly disdain for
these legends, until my third winter. I was
travelling through the Appolasian Plateau — that
dense, lightless forest highland entirely enclosed
by vast, near-impassable mountain ranges. It is a
notoriously ill-omened country, a known hotspot
where the veil of the world is dangerously thin and
the unseen world constantly presses against the
minds of men.

My horse had thrown me down a shale-bank during a
sudden alpine squall, shattering my thigh. I would
have belonged to the wolves had I not been dragged
into a hovel by a mountain hermit.

He was a man utterly crude and uncouth, barely
civilised, living in a filth that spoke of decades
without the tempering company of his fellow man. He
possessed a strange, practical intelligence — how
could he not, living by himself in such cursed
ground? He set my bone with rough precision and
bound it in clean poultices — yet from the moment I
woke in his shelter, I found him entirely
unendurable.

It was a wretched, ungrateful impulse. Every
logical faculty told me I owed this man my life,
yet his very presence filled my chest with a
suffocating, irrational hostility. I told myself it
was his coarse manners, his foul breath, and his
total lack of polite reserve that repulsed me. I
rationalised my sickness as the lingering trauma of
the fever. Yet were it not for my wound, I'd have
sooner faced the dark woods alone than share a fire
with that thing.

Some months passed. Forced into proximity, I came
to learn this man could speak. We conversed with
some difficulty; my mastery of the difficult
Syllvani dialect and his primitive development made
his company all the more difficult.

But on our fourth night together, when the weather
cleared, and the False Moon rose, throwing its
irregular, sickly light across the dense canopy and
hanging over us like the headman's axe, I noticed
something.

In those specific highlands, when that celestial
anomaly looms, the shadows are known to creep. The
air usually grows thick, and men hear the whispers
of things that do not belong to steel or stone. It
is a place where a man's thoughts are never
entirely his own. Yet, sitting in that paltry
lean-to, I realised the woods had gone completely,
terrifyingly dead. The darkness did not press
against our fire. The shadows remained mere
geometric lines on the dirt, fixed and unmoving
under the cast of the light. The heavy, whispering
background hum of the plateau had been utterly
choked out, replaced by a vacuum centred entirely
on the old man scrubbing a cooking pot and cursing
when he stubbed his toe.

He never spoke to me of what he was in any detail.
He likely did not know. Even his given name was
elusive to him; his name I learned was likely
Idras, inferred from him calling himself Id.

He merely sat in his chosen exile, distant and
contemplating, entirely isolated from a world that
could not love him if they tried. We parted when
the Thaw came, and I left him a purse of coin he
did not seem to want.

I write these lines now from the comfort of a quiet
library, yet the remorse remains like a scar upon
my spirit. I hated him for his lack of a soul,
while he, with his crude and clumsy hands, showed
me the only charity I found in those mountains. We
are cruel, governed by the invisible strings of a
sky we do not understand, and I fear our pity for
the Atraxi is nothing compared to the quiet,
unyielding tragedy of their days.

META — THE TRAIT
--------------------------------------------------

Still Born. Spoken: Atraxi. The empty cradle.
Not implemented. These notes are the start.

Tiers exist. Not locked.

  Minor
    Can live as a reclusive loner inside the
    bounds of civilisation.
    −30 Fellowship.
    The Mother’s Chill is the birth sign.
    Most die in the cradle. The ones who do not
    are the ones a settlement can almost bear.

  Pronounced
    Idras is this. The presence is unendurable
    without a wound or a duty that forces the
    company. Exile is the ordinary end.
    The number is not locked.

  Rumor
    Some are said to drive a man into irrational
    fear or frenzy by being present.
    Not confirmed. Not a number.

The effect on the Unveiling is observed, not
ruled. In Idras’s case, under the False Moon on
the Appolasian Plateau, the press of the veil
died in his presence. Shadows stayed shadows.
The hum stopped. How far that reaches, what it
costs, and whether it is a dispel or a blank
spot, is not written.

Do not invent the mechanic until the trait is
locked. The Mother’s Chill, the census word
Atraxi, and the Idras winter are the evidence.
The rest is tavern.
