# Processed Goods – Base Unit: 1 kg (or 1 litre where noted)

HEADER NOTE
All prices use the universal currency (1 gc = 20 sc = 240 bc).
Prices shown are base market values and are subject to ±50% (or greater) fluctuation by region, quality, supply, and demand.
Base unit is 1 kg wherever practical. Liquids use 1 litre (≈ 1 kg).
Larger lots are computed from the per-kg / per-litre base (1 sc = 12 bc).

Processed goods normally sell for 2.5–6× the value of their primary inputs after labour and wastage, except high-skill or luxury items.
A 1 kg scrap of a processed good is only worth its price if a workshop can use it.

Bulk is packed density for the good in its listed packing.
A vehicle is full at whichever comes first: weight or volume.
Standard cart: 800 kg or 2.5 m³
Heavy cart: 1,500 kg or 4 m³
River timber barge: 12,000 kg or 30 m³
A “cart load” is one standard cart.
A “heavy-cart load” is one heavy cart.
A “barge-load” is one river timber barge.

Packing
The Bulk figure already includes the listed packing.
Use the modifiers below only when the good is packed differently from its listed method.

Sacks
+2% weight. +5% volume.
Default for sack-goods.

Baskets
+15–25% volume.
Default for pottery and anything that must breathe or not be crushed.

Casks / Crates
+8–12% weight.
Volume is the cask’s outside measure.
Default for beer, wine, oil, and other liquids.

Loose pile
No extra penalty.
Default for ingots, planks, beams, and stacked stone-ware.

Woodwork
Timber sawn, split, hewn, or selected for use.
About a third to half of a log is lost to slab, bark, and sawdust.
The input is always a Forestry log or selected trunk of that species.
A piece too short or shaken for the named use drops to mixed planks, or to firewood.
Packed planks and beams: Bulk 500 kg/m³, loose-stacked.
Shingles: Bulk 350 kg/m³, bundled.

FORMS

Planks / Boards
Input: A sound log, bark on or roughly barked.
Labour: Saw-pit or mill. Two men and a saw, or a water-saw. Then stacking to season.
Use: Floors, walls, hull-skin, doors, chests, common furniture.
Property: Even thickness. Short offcuts are not shafts.

Roof shingles
Input: Straight-grained log, usually pine or oak.
Labour: Split with froe and mallet, then dressed thin.
Use: Roofs, sometimes wall-hang.
Property: Thin, overlapping, sheds water.

Spar / pole blank
Input: A young, straight pine or fir, or a clean top-log.
Labour: Felled whole, barked, knots dressed off.
Use: Scaffold-poles, light spars, ladder-stiles.
Property: Still one stick.

Hewn beam
Input: A large, reasonably straight log.
Labour: Scored and hewn square with axe and adze.
Use: Posts, ties, plates, hall and barn frames.
Property: One tree, squared.

Turning blanks
Input: Birch or beech, clean and even.
Labour: Cross-cut and split or sawn to a short block, then seasoned.
Use: Bowls, spoons, legs, handles.
Property: Short grain is acceptable.

Wet-work blanks
Input: Alder from river or fen.
Labour: Cut to length and kept from checking.
Use: Clogs, sluice-linings, cheap damp joinery.
Property: Stands wet. Not a structural timber.

Hub / pipe blanks
Input: Elm, the twisted, knot-free heart of a good log.
Labour: Selected and cross-cut.
Use: Wheel-hubs, pump-pipes.
Property: Interlocked grain. Hard to split.

Shaft / oar / handle blanks
Input: Straight-grained ash, one stick.
Labour: Selected and seasoned. Turning to the haft comes later.
Use: Axe and pollaxe shafts, spear-hafts, oars, tool-handles.
Property: Long spring. A board will not do.

Cooper’s staves
Input: Oak, split from the log rather than sawn when possible.
Labour: Split, dressed to a taper, seasoned.
Use: Casks, barrels, vats.
Property: Holds liquid when the grain runs right.

Knee / compass-timber blank
Input: An oak that grew a natural bend.
Labour: Found more than made.
Use: Ship-knees, frames.
Property: The strength is the grown curve.

Metal
Ore reduced and refined. Each step costs more than the last.
Ingots stack dense. Bulk: 4,000 kg/m³. Packing: loose pile.
Carts always fill by weight.

  Crude Iron
  Bloom or rough pig from the furnace.
  Made from iron ore or bog iron.
  Used only to be worked again.
  Base: 24 bc per kg
  Single ingot (1 kg) → 2 sc
  Ingot lot (50 kg) → 100 sc
  Cart load (800 kg) → 1,600 sc
  Heavy-cart load (1,500 kg) → 3,000 sc

  Wrought Iron
  Hammered, usable iron.
  Made from crude iron by smith’s labour.
  Used for tools, fittings, and common arms.
  Base: 50 bc per kg
  Single ingot (1 kg) → 4 sc 2 bc
  Ingot lot (50 kg) → 208 sc 4 bc
  Cart load (800 kg) → 3,333 sc 4 bc
  Heavy-cart load (1,500 kg) → 6,250 sc

  Steel
  Hardened, refined iron.
  Made from wrought iron with more fire and skill.
  Used for good edges and better arms.
  Base: 6 sc per kg
  Single ingot (1 kg) → 6 sc
  Ingot lot (50 kg) → 300 sc
  Cart load (800 kg) → 4,800 sc
  Heavy-cart load (1,500 kg) → 9,000 sc

  Fine Steel
  Clean, even steel for the best common work.
  Made from steel by specialist smiths.
  Used for fine blades and precision tools.
  Base: 12 sc per kg
  Single ingot (1 kg) → 12 sc
  Ingot lot (30 kg) → 360 sc
  Cart load (800 kg) → 9,600 sc

  Crucible Steel
  Rare, very even steel.
  Made from fine steel by restricted, costly refinement.
  Used for exceptional blades and prized work.
  Base: 12 gc per kg
  Single ingot (1 kg) → 12 gc
  Small lot (5 kg) → 60 gc

  Note on metal scrap
  Any piece that can be melted or faggoted keeps value.
  Price scrap as a fraction of the parent metal’s per-kg rate.

  Clean, sorted scrap of one metal: 50–70% of that metal’s ingot price
  Rusty but still one metal: 35–50%
  Mixed or dirty scrap: 20–35% of the cheapest metal in the heap
  Precious or steel scrap keeps its own ladder.

  Examples, per kg
  Clean wrought-iron scrap → 25–35 bc
  Rusty nails and offcuts → 18–25 bc
  Mixed iron heap → 10–18 bc
  Clean steel scrap → 3–4 sc
